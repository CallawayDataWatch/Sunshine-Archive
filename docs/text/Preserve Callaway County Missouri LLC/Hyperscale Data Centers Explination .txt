Introduction 

Data centers are large buildings full of computers that store data and run apps, 
websites, cloud services, and Al. A hyperscale data center is one of the biggest kinds, 
often more than 100,000 square feet and drawing more than 100 megawatts of 
power. Hundreds of them are being built or expanded across the U.S. 

The public debate is loud, and both sides reach for their strongest numbers. This guide 
tries to do something different: lay out what the evidence actually shows, what genuinely 
depends on local choices, and what is still unsettled. Where studies disagree, that 
disagreement is the point. 

Two choices end up shaping almost everything below: where a data center gets its 
electricity, and where it is built. Keep those two in mind as you read, because they 
come back in nearly every section, and together they explain why one facility can be a 
quiet, low-impact neighbor while another a few states away is a constant problem. 

One note before the numbers. Even the count of U.S. data centers depends on who is 
counting and how they define one. Estimates in 2025 ranged from about 3,600 (the 
National League of Cities, which counted at least 600 as hyperscale) to over 5,400 (the 
Environmental and Energy Study Institute). 1 Definitions matter throughout this topic, so 
the figures here are best read as ranges, not precise truths. 

Electricity use 

Start with the most basic question: how much power do these buildings actually use? 

Because they run around the clock, even a single large campus pulls steady electricity 
on the scale of a mid-sized city, and many are arriving on the grid at once. The U.S. 

power system was built for slow, predictable growth, so a fast wave of huge new users 
raises real questions about whether there is enough supply and who pays to build 

more.

overstate what actually gets constructed. 

Where the new power comes from 
All of this raises an obvious question: where will the electricity actually come from? In 
the near term, the answer is mostly the existing grid, often backed by new natural gas 
plants or on-site gas turbines, which is part of why emissions and noise come up later in 
this guide. A faster clean option that a few companies are using is restarting or 
expanding an existing nuclear plant. The deal to restart a reactor at Three Mile Island, 
renamed the Crane Clean Energy Center, to power Microsoft data centers is the headline 
example. Reusing a plant that already operated is quicker than building from scratch, 
since the work is mainly relicensing and refurbishment. But "quicker" is relative. The

Your electricity bill 

The short version: nationally there is no clear evidence that data centers are pushing 
up household electricity bills, but in a few fast-growing regions they may be adding 
real cost, and the heart of the fight is now over who pays to expand the grid. 

Using a lot of power is one thing. Making your monthly bill go up is another, and it is the 
question people ask most. It is also where the research splits hardest, often along the 
lines of who paid for it, so this section is worth reading slowly. 
The backdrop is real: residential electricity prices rose about 11.5% in 2025, outpacing 
inflation, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration.rn When that 
happens while data centers are going up nearby, people naturally connect the two. 
But the studies do not all agree that data centers are the cause. Several find no clear 
nationwide link. An analysis by the firm E3 for Virginia's legislature found no evidence 
that data centers had shifted costs onto households in the world's largest data center 
market." The Institute for Energy Research found no statistically significant connection 
between how many data centers a state has and its electricity prices.i2 The logic is that a 
grid's fixed costs get spread across more electricity sold, which can hold per-unit rates 
down or even lower them. 
Other evidence points to genuine pressure in specific regions. In the PJM grid (which 
includes Virginia), E3's own work attributed about half of a recent jump in "capacity" 
prices to load growth, with the rest coming from power-plant retirements and market

Put plainly: there is not strong evidence that data centers are the primary driver of rising

Virginia, where rapid data center demand is contributing to new generation, 
transmission, and capacity costs alongside other pressures like plant retirements, 
inflation, and grid modernization. The debate is increasingly less about whether data 
centers use enormous amounts of power, which no one disputes, than about who 
ultimately pays for the infrastructure that growth requires. Read the competing studies 
with their backers in mind: some of the reassuring ones were funded by the industry 
(E3's later work was commissioned by Amazon),'5 while some of the sharpest alarms 
come from advocacy groups and politicians. 

Water use 

Electricity is not the only resource a data center draws from the community. Most 
also use water, and unlike electricity, water is intensely local, so even a modest 
national total can strain one town's supply. The key distinction is between 
withdrawal (how much water a site takes in) and consumption (how much does not 
come back, mostly what evaporates). Consumption is the number that actually 
shrinks local supply. 

Large sites can consume 1 to 5 million gallons a day; five million gallons a day is roughly 
the water use of a town of 10,000 to 50,000 people.'6 Google reported that its largest 
U.S. site, in Council Bluffs, Iowa, withdrew about 3.9 million gallons and consumed 
about 2.8 million gallons per day.17 Looking ahead, the Berkeley Lab report projects total

Here is the piece most arguments leave out: data centers also use water indirectly, 
through the power plants that supply their electricity. Coal and gas plants evaporate 
large amounts of water to make steam.'9 For a center on a fossil-heavy grid, this hidden 
water use can rival or exceed what the building uses on-site. So a facility that boasts 
about low on-site water may still be driving heavy water use upstream. Solar and wind 
power are the main exceptions, since they use very little water to run. Nuclear is not an 
exception: it is low-carbon, but it relies on the same kind of steam cycle as fossil plants 
and can be just as water-intensive. This is the first place that "where the power comes 
from" really bites. 

For perspective, individual Al use is tiny: researchers estimate about a water-bottle's 
worth (roughly 0.16 gallons) per 30 minutes of AI use.2° The real concern is 
concentration. Millions of gallons pulled from one local system, especially on hot 
summer days when demand peaks and water is already tight, matter far more than the 
per-query amount. Industry comparisons to golf courses, car washes, or farming are 
sometimes accurate in raw volume, but they can mislead, because the issue is usually 
that local peak-day strain, not the national total. 

Water quality, wastewater, and aquifers 
Most of this section has been about how much water disappears. Two related worries go 
further: what the leftover water contains, and whether heavy pumping can drain 
underground supplies. They are often blended together in public debate, but they are 
separate issues. 

Start with quantity. Where a data center draws from groundwater rather than a city pipe, 
sustained pumping can lower the local water table and affect neighboring wells. In dry 
regions such as Arizona, heavy reliance on aquifers has already contributed to wells 
running low, which is one reason some operators are shifting to recycled water.21 The

"Closed-loop" sounds like it solves the water problem, and for on-site water it largely 
does. But the heat and electricity have to go somewhere. The extra power it needs, if it 
comes from a water-cooled fossil plant, simply moves the water use upstream rather 
than ending it. So a closed-loop facility is not water-free. It has traded direct water use 
for higher energy use and indirect water use. Critics argue the term is used to make sites 
look greener than they are;27 engineers counter that, paired with clean power, closed-
loop genuinely lowers total water draw. Both can be true depending on the grid behind it. 

There is also a newer wrinkle: local heat. Because closed-loop and air-cooled systems 
reject heat into the air instead of into evaporated water, that heat lands on the 
surrounding neighborhood. In 2026, Arizona State University researchers published 
what they describe as the first peer-reviewed field measurements of this effect, driving 
sensors upwind and downwind of four Phoenix-area facilities. They found downwind air 
temperatures running on average about 1.3 to 1.6°F warmer than upwind, with peaks up 
to roughly 4°F, detectable as far as a third of a mile away. The cause is air-cooled 
condensers that discharge air 14 to 25°F above the surrounding temperature, with one

Tax breaks and incentives 

So far the costs have been physical: power, water, heat. There is a financial one that 
is easy to miss, namely the deals communities strike to attract these centers in the 
first place. States and towns often offer sales-tax exemptions on equipment, 
property-tax abatements, and sometimes discounted power, all to win the project. 
Because data centers create few permanent jobs once built, often only dozens to a 
few hundred per facility once the large but temporary construction crew leaves, 
residents reasonably ask whether the public gets a fair return. 

The advocacy watchdog Good Jobs First, which opposes most of these subsidies, found 
in an earlier study that incentives averaged about $1.95 million per job across 11 projects, 
with one Apple deal in North Carolina reaching $6.4 million per job.29 Newer state data 
tells a similar story. In Illinois, subsidized centers drew over $5.4 billion in investment 
but created 339 jobs, about $1.4 million each.3° In Virginia, the data center sales-tax 
exemption cost roughly $1 billion in 2024, about 81% of the state's entire economic-
development incentive budget, and it is uncapped.3i Georgia, Virginia, and Texas each

Supporters make a fair counterpoint. The real payoff, they argue, is not jobs but a bigger 
tax base, since large investments fund local budgets even at a discount, and incentives 
are rarely the deciding factor in where a center lands anyway. In one industry survey, 
only 3% of data center owners called tax credits their biggest siting factor; land and 
power costs mattered far more.34 So the strongest criticism is not that every incentive is 
bad, but that many are automatic, uncapped, permanent, and poorly disclosed, so the 
public often cannot even see what a deal costs.35 On that last point about weak 
disclosure, reviewers across the political spectrum tend to agree.

have quietly worked on it for a year or more.

NDA.38 Microsoft, for its part, says it uses confidentiality agreements early on to protect 
commercial information, address security, and comply with permitting, and officials note 
that the final votes still happen in public meetings.39 

A couple of points help keep the concern accurate. Signing an NDA is usually a 
negotiated condition rather than something imposed on officials. A company makes 
confidentiality part of the deal for continuing to consider a site, and local leaders weigh 
accepting those terms against the risk of losing the project to another town. People can 
reasonably disagree about whether officials should agree to that, but it is a tradeoff they 
enter into, and describing it that way keeps the focus where it belongs. The deeper issue 
is not that secrecy exists at all, since early-stage development is often quiet for any large 
project. It is the mismatch between that secrecy and the stakes. These projects reshape a 
community's land, power, water, taxes, and environment, yet residents may get little 
time to weigh in before the decision has momentum. Tellingly, the same transparency 
gap appears even where no NDA is signed. In DeForest, Wisconsin, officials worked on a 
$12 billion proposal for months before announcing it.4c) So the strongest version of the 
concern is about disclosure and timing, with NDAs as the sharpest example rather than 
the whole story. In response, several states, including Wisconsin, have introduced bills to 
ban or limit data center NDAs and require earlier public notice.41

Climate and emissions 

Zoom out from the local site to the planet. A data center's climate impact is almost 
entirely secondhand: the building itself emits little, but the power plants behind it 
can emit a lot. This is "where the power comes from" again, now measured in carbon. 

A 2024 analysis found the average data center in its sample had a carbon intensity of 
about 548 grams of CO2 per kilowatt-hour, roughly 50% higher than the U.S. average of 
369, mainly because many sit in fossil-heavy grids. In Virginia's PJM grid, about 61% of 
electricity comes from fossil fuels.42 A growing number of sites also add on-site natural 
gas turbines or diesel backup generators, which bring their own air pollution, such as 
nitrogen oxides and fine particles, a local health concern separate from carbon.43 

So the same building can be near carbon-free or quite dirty depending entirely on its 
power source. One running on nuclear, hydro, solar, or wind has a small footprint; one 
drawing from coal or gas has a large one. The International Energy Agency projects data 
center emissions may level off around 2030 as grids get cleaner, though that is a forecast, 
not a promise.44 

One claim deserves a closer look. Companies often say they are "100% renewable." This 
usually means they buy enough renewable-energy contracts or credits to match their 
yearly use, not that clean power flows to the building every hour. Whether that actually 
cuts emissions depends on whether the purchases add new clean generation to the grid 
or just take credit for power that already existed. That is a genuine, unresolved debate 
among experts, not a settled fact in either direction.

building appears where something else used to be. A single hyperscale campus can 
cover hundreds to over a thousand acres.

On health, it pays to be careful with the numbers. One 2025 model estimated that, by 
2030, the air pollution tied to data centers, mainly from fossil power and on-site 
generators, could contribute to roughly 1,300 premature deaths a year and about 820 
billion in health costs.47 That is a single modeled projection, not a measured outcome, 
and it leans heavily on assumptions about how the centers are powered. It is worth 
knowing, but it should not be treated as an established fact. 

Noise 

Once a center is running, the complaint neighbors raise most is not abstract. It is the 
sound. Data centers make noise from cooling fans that run constantly, backup diesel 
generators tested periodically, and, increasingly, on-site gas turbines that run 
nonstop. 

Individual generators run around 85 to 100 decibels, and facilities run several at once; 
for reference, sustained levels above about 70 decibels are considered the upper edge of 
safe.48 The sound carries hundreds of feet. Much of the complaint is about low-frequency 
hum and infrasound, which neighbors describe as a constant vibration and which is hard 
to capture on a standard decibel meter, which in turn makes it hard to prove and harder 
to regulate.49

Light pollution 

The other thing neighbors notice after dark is the light. Large sites use bright security 
lighting all night and often glow visibly across the landscape. 

Residents near rural sites describe data centers that "glow like a city of lights" and are 
visible for miles, washing out dark night skies.52 The impact is much smaller where a 
center sits near already-lit development like an airport or industrial zone,53 so once 
again, siting drives the outcome. 
All-night lighting can disrupt sleep, affect wildlife, and harm stargazing, concerns well 
established for outdoor lighting in general, and much of it is preventable with shielded, 
downward-pointing fixtures.54 That said, compared with electricity and water, light 
pollution from data centers specifically is the least studied and least measured impact in 
this guide, so the claims here rest more on resident reports than on hard data.
